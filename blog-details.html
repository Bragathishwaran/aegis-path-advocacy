5 Things Every Parent Must Know Before Walking Into an IEP Meeting

School IEP meetings can feel like sitting in an interrogation room with 8 professionals facing you. Here is the legal playbook to turn that dynamic into an equal partnership.

AO

Adaeze Okafor

Founder & Former Special Education Director · Special Ed Advocate

When you walk into an Individualized Education Program (IEP) meeting, you are officially defined under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) as an equal and mandatory member of the team. Yet, most parents leave feeling rushed, overwhelmed, or pressured into signing documents they barely had time to read.

1. You Have the Right to Receive Drafts & Testing in Advance

Never allow a school psychologist or special education teacher to read an evaluation report to you for the first time at the conference table. Under IDEA § 300.501 and standard procedural guidelines, parents have the right to inspect and review all educational records prior to any meeting.

Always send a written request at least 5 business days prior asking for draft present levels, proposed goals, and full psychometric test protocols. This allows you to digest the scores, confer with private therapists, and prepare informed questions.

Advocate Tip:

Send an email: "Please provide complete drafts of all evaluations, progress logs, and proposed IEP goals 5 school days prior to our scheduled IEP date so that we can participate meaningfully as equal team members."

2. Present Levels (PLAAFP) Drive Everything Else

The most critical section of the IEP is not the goals page; it is the Present Levels of Academic Achievement and Functional Performance (PLAAFP). If a child’s sensory dysregulation or reading phonemic gap is omitted from the present levels, the school has zero obligation to draft goals or assign resource minutes for it.

Audit the PLAAFP line-by-line. Does it accurately state where your child is currently performing with objective data, rather than subjective opinions like "Johnny is doing nicely"?

3. Goals Must Be SMART and Connected to Baselines

Every IEP goal must be Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound. A goal stating "Ava will improve reading fluency with 80% accuracy" is legally deficient because it fails to specify the starting baseline, the testing tool, or the conditions of measurement.

Ensure each goal specifies:

  • The starting baseline (e.g., "From a baseline of 42 words per minute at 3rd grade level...")
  • The ending benchmark target (e.g., "...to 85 words per minute...")
  • How progress will be objectively measured (e.g., "as measured by 3 consecutive weekly curriculum-based probes.")

4. Accommodations vs. Modifications: Know the Line

An accommodation changes how a student learns or demonstrates knowledge (e.g., speech-to-text software, frequent sensory breaks, reduced visual clutter). A modification changes what the student is expected to learn (e.g., altering the curriculum below grade level).

Unintended modifications can jeopardize a student’s eligibility for a standard high school diploma. Always confirm that accommodations are explicitly written with exact frequency, duration, and designated personnel.

5. You Never Have to Sign the IEP on the Spot

School staff may hand you a signature page at the end of the meeting and ask you to sign immediately. In virtually every state, signing to acknowledge attendance is separate from signing consent for implementation.

Politely say: "Thank you for this productive discussion. As an equal partner on this team, I will take this draft home, review it thoroughly alongside my notes, and return our written response within 5 school days."
